How to Spot a Spam or Scam Call Before You Pick Up: Common Red Flags in US Numbers

Identifying Warning Signs in U.S. Numbers

You see a number on your phone. The number appears to be from your neighborhood or city. After a brief hesitation, you pick up the call. "Hi! This is the I.R.S.," says a male voice. "There is a problem with your tax account." Click! You've just become part of the millions of Americans receiving monthly spam/scams.

Fortunately, most of these calls tip off the caller before he/she has spoken. The phone number on your screen, the timing of the call and/or ringing behavior can give you early warning signs of potential problems. Below are some things to watch for.

Warning signs within the number

1. It looks very much like your number

You notice a call coming in from a number which matches your area code and the next three digits. That is known as neighbor spoofing. They are spoofing your number to fool people into answering the call due to familiarity. Do you have anybody you would know with a number that is similar to your own? If not, beware. Local callers do exist; however, there is little chance that you know somebody with a number that is nearly identical to your own.

2. The area code does not match anywhere in your past.

You never lived in Atlanta. You do not have relatives in Atlanta. And you did not apply for employment in Atlanta. Then why is there a 404 calling you?

While an area code that does not appear in your history does not necessarily mean the call is a scam (people retain their cellular telephone numbers after moving), the combination of an unfamiliar area code and other warning signs listed below makes it important to consider. To find out about all area codes and overlays, visit the official North America Numbering Plan Administrator website at nationalnanpa.com.

3. It is a toll-free number you did not anticipate.

Toll-free numbers begin with the prefixes 800, 833, 844, 855, 866, 877 or 888. Legitimate business and companies use toll-free numbers frequently. However, so do scam artists and fake "support" lines. If you have purchased nothing recently, established an account or contacted a company, an unsolicited toll-free call merits a closer inspection.

4. It is an unfamiliar area code.

Some scams utilize numbers from abroad with an area code that resembles a U.S. area code. Some Caribbean area codes are formatted in the same manner as U.S. area codes making them easily confused with a call from inside the country. If you identify an unfamiliar area code, research it before returning a call. Returning a call to an incorrect number could result in additional charges to your account for premium rates.

5. There are numerous complaints associated with the number.

It's simple to perform a search for a number. Type the number in quotes and enter it into a search engine, such as Google. Use a reverse phone lookup tool to perform an immediate search on the number. If multiple individuals have identified it as a robocall or scam, that means you have made contact with one. A large amount of independent complaints regarding the same number is indicative.

Warning signs related to how the call operates

6. It rings once and then stops.

That is how telemarketing scams work. Ring once and stop. You feel compelled to call back and hopefully find out who was on the other end. Unfortunately, you probably were connected to a premium rate line where you would be charged per minute for each conversation. Be cautious of single-ring calls from numbers you cannot identify.

7. It occurs at an abnormal time.

A call at 6:45 AM or 9:30 PM from an unidentified number is unlikely to occur from legitimate businesses. Telemarketers are restricted by law in terms of the times during which they may call consumers. While the odd timing alone does not constitute evidence of illegitimacy, it does add another warning sign.

8. You receive multiple calls from the same number in rapid succession.

Multiple calls from the same number in a short period of time without leaving voicemail typically indicate an auto-dialer going through a list or an individual attempting to verify whether your number is active. People with genuine messages to deliver tend to leave one message and wait for your response.

9. There is a pause while you are being connected.

When you answer, you say hello and experience a pause -- either silence, a click, etc., before hearing someone speaking. This pause is typical of automated dialing systems and indicates they are connecting you to either a live representative or beginning to play a recorded message. If you experience pauses when answering a call, simply hang up.

Warning signs once you pick up (or listen to voicemail)

If you answer, or receive a voicemail message, these are signals that suggest "SCAM":

  • Pressure/Urgency: "Do something NOW otherwise your account will be closed." Genuine organizations provide reasonable amounts of time prior to taking action against your account.
  • Threats: Threats of legal actions (e.g., arrest), deportation, freezing of your assets/account(s). No government agency contacts you threateningly via phone.
  • Unusual Payment Requests: Scammers' favorite ways include asking for payment using gift cards, wire transfer services, cryptocurrencies (like Bitcoin) or mobile payment applications (such as Venmo). Legitimate organizations will not ask for payment for bills (even ones over $500) in gift cards.
  • Request for Personal Details: Never share your SSN, bank login credentials or one-time passcodes with someone who called you.
  • Prize You Did Not Enter For: Someone claims you won (prize) and asks for a nominal fee to release funds. Classic!
  • Robo-voice/Recorded Voice: Someone you expected to speak with personally calls robotically or leaves pre-recorded messages and did not expect that nor asked for it.

Hang up immediately if any of these are present. You do not owe anyone a nice goodbye when you realize the caller is a scammer.
